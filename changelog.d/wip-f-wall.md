- A person who is shared with an agent at Use now gets terminals of their own on its computer, as a Unix user of their own with a home that only they can open. The agent's own home, shell history, running programs' environment and tmux sessions are closed to them by the computer itself, whatever the command says. Their next command is refused, and their programs and terminals there end, as soon as their share is taken away.
- On a shared agent's computer a person reaches the projects they are on, at their project role (edit for a Lead or a Member, read for a Reviewer), and what Files lets them see, and nothing else of the agent's home. Who is on a project is read from the people who are on it directly: a person who is on it only through a group, or as an admin, gets no project folder in the terminal. The Files folder is opened whole or not at all. A change in who is on a project reaches a person at their next command, within five seconds; a file made before a person joined a project stays unreadable to them. A Space with no room for the folder or the access lists says so in a sentence and the command does not run.
- The terminal list shows another person's terminal on an agent's computer as one row that says it is there: no folder, no chat, no name, no one at the keyboard, and its screen, last line and last command are never given. Nobody types in or reads a terminal that is in another person's space, the agent's owner and admins included.
- The Computer tab says whose side of a shared agent's computer it shows ("Your own space on <agent>'s computer."), draws only your own terminals, and counts the terminals of other people without listing them.
- Each person who does not own a shared agent browses in a browser profile of their own, so a site someone else signed in to is not signed in for them; the owner keeps the agent's own profile. The browser picture, the live view and take-over use the profile of the person asking. A new question, computers.browser.my_sign_ins, lists which sites your own turns of an agent are signed in to.
- In a chat with other people, each person's message runs for that person: the agent works as their own user on a shared agent's computer and in their own folder for the chat, even when another person's account or a key of the Space pays for the turn. Who pays is counted where it was. The chat has one shared folder on the agent's computer, reached as ~/chat by everyone who works in it.
- What of one computer is still shared between people who use a shared agent, and cannot be closed without more rights than a workspace is given: a person sees the command lines of other people's programs (do not pass a secret as an argument), reaches web servers other people run on the computer's own address (127.0.0.1), and shares one processor, memory and process budget with them, so one person can slow the others. A person with write on a project can put a script in it that the agent, which runs as the agent's owner's user, may run: giving write on a project is trusting that person with what the agent can do.
- A person who leaves the Space has what they made on a shared agent's computer erased at once; a person who loses the use of an agent keeps their home there for 30 days, and it is theirs again if they are shared with it before. A command that only names a folder such as ~/.ssh is no longer taken for a message sent to the outside, and whoami, id and getent passwd answer for a person in their own terminal.
- The Computer tab on an agent's page now knows who is looking: your own terminals read "Your terminals" and another person's carry their name, instead of "Someone terminals".
- The agent's home on a shared computer cannot be walked by the people who use the agent, including files written with a loose umask by an older workspace; nobody can make another person's folder first, a person keeps one user number for the whole Space, and a person who loses the use of an agent in any way (a group, a role, an expiry, a suspension) is out of its computer within seconds. A person who leaves a chat loses its shared folder. A person with no access is told so in one sentence and sees nothing of the agent's terminals.
